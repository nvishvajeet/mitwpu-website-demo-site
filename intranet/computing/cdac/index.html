Computing Resources Centre

Remote access to C-DAC

Research computing beyond what a laboratory machine can do, on systems the university does not own and reaches over the network. The arrangement is being made; the account, the address and the terms are not settled, so what follows is the shape of the work rather than the instructions for it.

Read this first

Every instruction on this page is a sample

The university has published no arrangement with C-DAC, and this page describes none. What is here is the shape remote access to a national computing facility takes as such services are generally documented: you are given an account, you register a public key, you connect over SSH, you move your data, and you submit work to a scheduler rather than running it on the machine you logged in to.

Every hostname, account name, path, queue and project code below is a placeholder written in CAPITALS inside angle brackets. None of them is real, none of them will resolve, and none should be tried. The real values arrive with the account, from whoever issues it — not from this page, and not from a colleague's recollection.

When the arrangement is settled this page is replaced by its instructions. Until then, the list at the foot of the page is what has to be decided, and it is the useful part.

When you need a machine you cannot walk up to

The threshold is not cleverness, it is arithmetic. Work belongs on a shared facility when it will not fit in the memory of a workstation, when it would take longer than you are prepared to leave a laptop running, or when it is the same calculation a few thousand times over with a different parameter each time. Everything below that threshold is faster on the machine you already have, because there is no queue in front of it.

What a facility of this kind gives you is not one large computer. It is a login machine you type on, a much larger set of compute machines you never log in to, a filesystem both can see, and a scheduler that decides when your work runs on the second set. Almost every mistake a first-time user makes comes from not yet believing the second sentence.

The login node
Where you land. For editing, moving files and submitting work — never for the work itself. It is shared by everyone logged in at that moment.
The compute nodes
Where the work actually runs, reached only through the scheduler. You do not choose one and you do not connect to it.
The scheduler
The queue. You describe what your job needs, it decides when that becomes available, and your job runs unattended when it does.
The allocation
The budget of machine time a project is granted. Work is charged against it, so a job that asks for eight times what it needs costs eight times as much.

What you will need before you apply

Three of these are yours to get ready now, and they are the reason this page is worth reading before there is anything to connect to. A key pair takes two minutes and is the thing most often missing when an account is issued.

An SSH key pair
Generated on the machine you will connect from. National facilities do not issue passwords for this, and the public half is what you send when you apply.
A working knowledge of the shell
You will be typing at a prompt with no windows on the far end. The introduction on this portal is enough to start with.
A project, and somebody answerable for it
Time on a national facility is granted to a piece of research rather than to a person, and normally needs a faculty member's name against it.
Data you may lawfully move
Anything with a person in it, anything under a licence that fixes where it may be held, and anything a collaborator gave you under terms — check before it leaves this university, not after.

Getting an account

In outline, and marked as such throughout. No step below has a route, an address or a form behind it yet.

Apply for an account on a national facility

At the end of this you have a username, a system to connect to, and a project your work is charged against.

Applies to Nobody yet. This is the shape of the process, not the process.

Before you start, you need

  • A public key you generated yourself, and a private half you have never sent to anyone.
  • A one-paragraph description of the work and roughly what it will need.
  • The name of the faculty member answerable for the project.

  1. Generate a key pair if you have none

    This part is real and you can do it today: the command is the same everywhere and does not depend on any arrangement being settled.

    Generate a key pair Sample. Everything in <ANGLE BRACKETS> is a placeholder for you to replace, and the values here are not this service's real ones. 

    # SAMPLE — placeholder values, not this service's real ones.# One key per person. The comment is only a label, so that you can tell your keys apart later.ssh-keygen -t ed25519 -C "<YOUR-NAME>-<YEAR>"

    Give it a passphrase. A private key with no passphrase is a password written on a note that also fits in a backup.

  2. Send the public half with the application

    The file ending in .pub, and nothing else. The other file is the private half and is never sent to anybody, including the people running the facility.

    Read the public half so you can paste it Sample. Everything in <ANGLE BRACKETS> is a placeholder for you to replace, and the values here are not this service's real ones. 

    # SAMPLE — placeholder values, not this service's real ones.cat ~/.ssh/id_ed25519.pub

    What you should see

    ssh-ed25519 AAAAC3NzaC1lZDI1NTE5AAAAI<...>  <YOUR-NAME>-<YEAR>

    One line, beginning ssh-ed25519 or ssh-rsa. If what you are looking at begins with five dashes and the word BEGIN, you have opened the private half; close it.

    This site will never publish a key, yours or anyone's, and neither should any page you are asked to paste one into.

  3. Wait for the account, and read what arrives

    What comes back is a username, the address of a login node, the name of your project, and the facility's own conditions of use. Those conditions are the ones that bind you; nothing on this page overrides them.

  4. Connect once, immediately

    Not because there is anything to do, but because an account that does not work is easiest to fix on the day it was issued and hardest three months later when the person who issued it has moved on.

Connecting

One command the first time, and one word afterwards. The second is worth setting up on the first day: an address you type twenty times a week is an address you eventually mistype into somebody else's machine.

Open a session on the login node Sample. Everything in <ANGLE BRACKETS> is a placeholder for you to replace, and the values here are not this service's real ones. 

# SAMPLE — placeholder values, not this service's real ones.ssh <YOUR-USERNAME>@<LOGIN-NODE-YOU-WERE-GIVEN>

What you should see

The authenticity of host '<LOGIN-NODE-YOU-WERE-GIVEN>' can't be established.ED25519 key fingerprint is SHA256:<FINGERPRINT-THE-HOST-OFFERS>.Are you sure you want to continue connecting (yes/no)?

Compare that fingerprint against the one the facility published before you answer yes. It is the only moment at which you can tell the real host from something standing in front of it, and it is the moment everybody skips.

Make it one word, in ~/.ssh/config on your own machine Sample. Everything in <ANGLE BRACKETS> is a placeholder for you to replace, and the values here are not this service's real ones. 

# SAMPLE — placeholder values, not this service's real ones.Host <SHORT-NAME-YOU-CHOOSE>    HostName <LOGIN-NODE-YOU-WERE-GIVEN>    User <YOUR-USERNAME>    IdentityFile ~/.ssh/id_ed25519# Then, from anywhere:ssh <SHORT-NAME-YOU-CHOOSE>

This file is read by ssh, scp and rsync alike, so the short name works for all three.

Moving data in and out

Two tools, and the second one is the one to learn. `scp` copies a file; `rsync` copies what is different, resumes when it is interrupted and can be run again safely — which matters when the thing being moved is two hundred gigabytes over a network somebody else is also using.

Before any of it: check what you are allowed to move. A shared national facility is not a place to discover that a dataset had terms on it.

Copy one file up Sample. Everything in <ANGLE BRACKETS> is a placeholder for you to replace, and the values here are not this service's real ones. 

# SAMPLE — placeholder values, not this service's real ones.scp <FILE-ON-YOUR-MACHINE> <SHORT-NAME-YOU-CHOOSE>:<PATH-ON-THE-FACILITY>

Copy a directory up, resumably Sample. Everything in <ANGLE BRACKETS> is a placeholder for you to replace, and the values here are not this service's real ones. 

# SAMPLE — placeholder values, not this service's real ones.# -a keeps permissions and timestamps, -v says what it is doing, --partial keeps what it managed before the connection dropped.rsync -av --partial <DIRECTORY-ON-YOUR-MACHINE>/ <SHORT-NAME-YOU-CHOOSE>:<PATH-ON-THE-FACILITY>/

The trailing slash on the source is not decoration: without it rsync copies the directory INTO the target rather than copying its contents.

Bring results back Sample. Everything in <ANGLE BRACKETS> is a placeholder for you to replace, and the values here are not this service's real ones. 

# SAMPLE — placeholder values, not this service's real ones.# Same command, the arguments the other way round.rsync -av --partial <SHORT-NAME-YOU-CHOOSE>:<RESULTS-PATH>/ <DIRECTORY-ON-YOUR-MACHINE>/

Bring results back as you go rather than at the end. Scratch filesystems on shared facilities are routinely cleared, and the notice period is not always long.

Running work: the scheduler

You do not run your calculation on the machine you logged in to. That machine is shared by everyone currently connected, and a job started on it slows all of them down and is usually killed. You write down what the work needs, hand that to the scheduler, and it runs when the machines are free.

Which scheduler this will be is not settled, and the commands differ between them. What does not differ is the four questions every one of them asks, and the answers are yours to work out before you can submit anything at all.

How many processors?
Asking for more than your program can use wastes the allocation and makes the wait longer, not shorter.
For how long?
An honest estimate plus a margin. Too short and the job is killed mid-run; too long and it queues behind everything.
How much memory?
The commonest cause of a job dying at three in the morning with nothing useful written down.
Which queue, and against which project?
Facilities separate short test runs from long ones, and charge the time to a project rather than to you.

Submit a job

The same four moves on every facility, whatever the scheduler is called.

Applies to Sample only — the directives and commands below are placeholders, and the real ones come with the account.

  1. Write a submission script

    A shell script with the scheduler's requests at the top and your work at the bottom. It is a file rather than a command line so that the run is reproducible and so that you can read, six months later, what you actually asked for.

    A submission script, in outline Sample. Everything in <ANGLE BRACKETS> is a placeholder for you to replace, and the values here are not this service's real ones. 

    # SAMPLE — placeholder values, not this service's real ones.#!/bin/bash# Every line beginning with the scheduler's directive marker is a request, not a comment.#<SCHEDULER-DIRECTIVE> --job-name=<SHORT-NAME>#<SCHEDULER-DIRECTIVE> --nodes=<HOW-MANY-NODES>#<SCHEDULER-DIRECTIVE> --time=<HOURS>:<MINUTES>:00#<SCHEDULER-DIRECTIVE> --account=<YOUR-PROJECT-CODE>#<SCHEDULER-DIRECTIVE> --output=<JOB-NAME>-%j.out# Load what your program needs. Never assume it is there by default.module load <SOFTWARE-THE-FACILITY-PROVIDES># And then the work itself.<YOUR-PROGRAM> <YOUR-INPUT>

    Write the output file name with the job number in it. Two runs that overwrite each other's output is a morning gone.

  2. Submit it, and note the number

    Hand the script to the scheduler Sample. Everything in <ANGLE BRACKETS> is a placeholder for you to replace, and the values here are not this service's real ones. 

    # SAMPLE — placeholder values, not this service's real ones.<SUBMIT-COMMAND> <YOUR-SCRIPT>.sh

    What you should see

    Submitted batch job <JOB-NUMBER>

    That number is how you refer to the run from now on — to check it, to cancel it, and to ask anybody about it.

  3. Check on it without watching it

    A queued job is not a broken one. Waiting is what a shared facility does, and a large request waits longer than a small one — which is an argument for asking for what you need rather than for what you would like.

    See where your work is in the queue Sample. Everything in <ANGLE BRACKETS> is a placeholder for you to replace, and the values here are not this service's real ones. 

    # SAMPLE — placeholder values, not this service's real ones.<QUEUE-COMMAND> -u <YOUR-USERNAME>

    Stop a run you no longer want Sample. Everything in <ANGLE BRACKETS> is a placeholder for you to replace, and the values here are not this service's real ones. 

    # SAMPLE — placeholder values, not this service's real ones.# Do this as soon as you know. A job you have stopped caring about is still spending the project's allocation.<CANCEL-COMMAND> <JOB-NUMBER>

  4. Read the output, then bring it home

    The output file the scheduler wrote is the first place to look when something went wrong, and it is usually the only place. Copy results back to storage you control rather than leaving them on a scratch filesystem.

    Test on a small input first, every time. A twelve-hour job that fails in its last minute because of a path typed wrongly is the standard first experience of a shared facility, and it is entirely avoidable.

The run is done when the output file stops growing and the job is no longer in the queue.

What must not go on to a facility the university does not own

A shared national system is a third party, and moving data to it is a disclosure whatever the intention. Four categories, and the first one is the one that gets people into real trouble.

This page carries no credential of any kind, and neither will its replacement: not a key, not a password, not a project code that acts as one. A page behind a sign-in is still a web page, and it gets bookmarked, screenshotted and pasted into wikis.

Anything with a person in it
Student records, patient data, survey responses that can be traced back, photographs of identifiable people. Ask before, not after.
Data whose licence fixes where it may be held
Common in collaborations and in purchased datasets, and rarely remembered at the moment of copying.
Software you are not licensed to run there
A licence for a machine in a laboratory here does not travel to a machine somewhere else.
Any secret at all
A key, a token or a password in a script is a secret on a shared filesystem that other people's jobs run beside.

What this page cannot yet tell you

Eight things nobody can guess, and the reason this page is samples rather than instructions.

One of them is worse than the others. Until somebody states what data may leave this university's systems, every researcher moving a dataset to a shared national facility is making that decision alone, on the day, with a deadline.
