Computing Resources Centre

Reaching university systems from off campus

A virtual private network puts the machine in front of you on the university's network, wherever that machine is — so the things that only work on campus work from home. The university is introducing one. It has published nothing about it, and this page says so rather than inventing it.

Read this first

This service is not running yet, and every command below is a sample

There is no published gateway address, no client to install, no account to enable and nobody to ask. What is here is the shape of the thing, so that a reader knows what they will be doing and what they will need when it arrives.

Every value in every command is a placeholder written in capitals inside angle brackets. None of them will connect to anything, and none should be tried. When the service is announced, this page is replaced by its instructions and the marks come off.

What a VPN does, and what it does not

It is a tunnel, not a permission. Your traffic goes to the university first and out from there, so a system that decides who you are by where you are coming from sees you as being on campus. That is the whole of the trick, and everything a VPN is good for follows from it.

What it is not is a way in for other people, a private network of your own, or anything to do with anonymity. It does not make a laptop safe, it does not encrypt what you do after the traffic leaves the university, and it is not a substitute for having an account on the thing you are trying to reach.

What it gives you
An address on the university's network, so services that check the address let you in.
What it does not give you
Access to anything you did not already have an account for. A tunnel is not an authorisation.
What it costs
Speed. Everything you do goes to Pune and back, including the things that did not need to.

The things that only work on campus

One of them is published and is the reason this page exists at all: the library's subscriptions are licensed by network address, so a database that opens in the reading room refuses you at home. Getting access

Nothing here is a process the university has published. It is the shape such a process takes, set out so that whoever writes the real one has a list to work against and so that a reader knows what they will be asked for.

Ask for VPN access

At the end of this you have an account enabled for the VPN and the details needed to configure a client.

Applies to Nobody yet. There is no route to make this request.

Before you start, you need

  • A working university sign-in. If yours does not work, that is a different problem and it is on the Centre's own page.
  • A reason: which system you need to reach from off campus. Access to a network is normally granted for a purpose rather than in general.

  1. Check whether you need it at all

    Most things a university publishes are on the open web and do not need a tunnel. It is worth two minutes: a VPN slows everything you do, and a request nobody needed to make is the most expensive kind.

  2. Make the request through whatever route the Centre publishes

    No such route is published. When one is, it belongs here, and it is the second item on this page's work list.

    Do not send a password, a key, or a photograph of a screen to anybody as part of a request. No legitimate request for network access needs any of them.

  3. Receive the details

    What arrives is an address to connect to, a name to connect as, and whichever second factor the university decides on. It is not a password sent in a message; a service that emails you a password is a service to raise a ticket about.

  4. Install the client the university names

    Take it from the address the Centre gives you and from nowhere else. VPN clients are among the most impersonated software there is, and the copy at the top of a search result is not the one you want.

    Which client this will be is not published. Until it is, treat any page claiming to be MIT-WPU's VPN download as a phishing page, because today that is all it could be.

You are on when a system that refused you a minute ago lets you in.

Connecting, once there is something to connect to

Two shapes, because universities publish two. Either the client is an application with a window, in which case there is nothing to type; or the tunnel is a command, in which case it is one line and can be scripted. Both are shown so that neither is a surprise.

Bring the tunnel up from a terminal Sample. Everything in <ANGLE BRACKETS> is a placeholder for you to replace, and the values here are not this service's real ones. 

# SAMPLE — placeholder values, not this service's real ones.# The address and the account name both arrive with your access; neither is published.sudo <VPN-CLIENT-COMMAND> --server <GATEWAY-YOU-WERE-GIVEN> --user <YOUR-USERNAME>

What you should see

Connected. Your address on the university network is <ADDRESS-ASSIGNED-TO-YOU>.

sudo is asking for the password of the machine in front of you, not for a university one. If something asks for your university password at this point, stop.

Check that it worked, before assuming it did Sample. Everything in <ANGLE BRACKETS> is a placeholder for you to replace, and the values here are not this service's real ones. 

# SAMPLE — placeholder values, not this service's real ones.# What the outside world now thinks your address is.curl -s <ADDRESS-ECHO-SERVICE-THE-CENTRE-NAMES># Whether the thing you actually wanted is now reachable.ping -c 3 <SYSTEM-YOU-ARE-TRYING-TO-REACH>

A tunnel that is up and a service that is reachable are two different questions, and answering only the first is how an afternoon goes.

Take it down when you have finished Sample. Everything in <ANGLE BRACKETS> is a placeholder for you to replace, and the values here are not this service's real ones. 

# SAMPLE — placeholder values, not this service's real ones.# Leaving it up sends everything you do through the university, including the things that are nobody's business there.sudo <VPN-CLIENT-COMMAND> --disconnect

What you are agreeing to when you connect

A VPN puts a machine the university does not own on to the network it is answerable for, so the terms matter more than they do for wireless in a corridor. None of these terms is published, and all five will have to be.

Two of them are worth stating now because they are true of every such service and cost somebody their access every year: the account is yours and is not lent, and the machine you connect from is your responsibility while it is on the network.

What this page cannot yet tell you

Seven decisions and one page. None of them can be guessed: a wrong gateway address is a support ticket, a wrong eligibility rule is somebody told they may not have a thing they may, and a wrong client is a person installing software from a stranger.

  • Service detail: A fact this page cannot state — Who is eligible for VPN access; The route by which access is requested; The gateway address, and the client software to use with it; Whether a second factor is required, and which; Which university systems the VPN is expected to reach; An acceptable-use statement, and what is logged; What happens to VPN access when a person leaves
  • Answer: A question nobody has answered yet — Who will be allowed to use the VPN?; What will the VPN let me reach that I cannot reach now?; Will it get me into the library's databases from home?; Which client will I install, and where do I get it?; Will I need a second factor, and what will it be?
