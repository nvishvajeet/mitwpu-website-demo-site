  3. World Peace Dome

Set on the picturesque Vishwarajbaug campus at Loni Kalbhor, on the outskirts of Pune, the World Peace Dome is conceived as a living site of interreligious dialogue. Its formal name is longer than the one most visitors use — the Philosopher Saint Shri Dnyaneshwara World Peace Prayer Hall and World Peace Library — and it was built to hold a single idea in stone and bronze: that eastern spiritual wisdom and western scientific enquiry belong to one family.

Beneath the dome sits a prayer and assembly hall; below that, a library. Ringing the interior stand fifty-four larger-than-life bronze statues of saints, scientists and philosophers — a company that runs, in the monument's own phrase, “from Saint Dnyaneshwar to Albert Einstein.” The whole structure is an attempt to make an argument visible: that knowledge, whatever its tradition, points the same way.

The world as one family

Concept

The dome is the work of Prof. Dr. Vishwanath D. Karad, the founder of the MIT group of institutions in Pune and of MIT World Peace University. He describes it as a “Centre of Wisdom, Knowledge Dissemination and Social Transformation,” dedicated to the public rather than to any one faith. Its guiding text is the Sanskrit phrase Vasudhaiva Kutumbakam — “the world is one family.”

The idea took root long before the foundations. On a visit to Alandi in 1987 — the town near Pune associated with the thirteenth-century saint Dnyaneshwar — Karad has said the notion of a monument to peace took root in his mind on the banks of the Indrayani river. He had founded the Maharashtra Institute of Technology in 1983; the university around it would take the name MIT World Peace University in 2017, the year before the dome was completed.

“Union of Science and Religion and Spirituality alone will bring Peace and Harmony to the entire Humanity.”
Swami Vivekananda — the sentence the monument was built to illustrate

The architecture

Grandeur

By the numbers the monument reports, it is a genuinely large structure. The dome measures 160 feet across; the building rises 263 feet from the ground to its apex, with the dome itself accounting for roughly the top 96 feet. Remarkably, the whole span rests on just twenty-four pillars. It is, on the project's own account, presently the world's largest dome — a claim best held lightly, but one supported by a straightforward comparison the monument invites (below).

The design is unashamedly symbolic. At the centre lies a meditation space intended to balance the seven chakras; around it, concentric rings of pillars carry meanings rather than loads alone. Twelve stand for the major world religions; twenty-four for the sciences that “define and steer life”; and, counted in full, one hundred and eight — a number long held sacred in Indian tradition — for the values of peace and sustainability. At the apex sits an idol of Saraswati, the goddess of knowledge.

The prayer hall and its painted sky

Under the dome

The hall built inside the dome can take some three thousand people, in a room shaped for stillness. Across the inner surface of the dome a sunrise is painted, and tiny openings let daylight through so that the illumination shifts through the day. Seated below, a visitor is surrounded by the statues of the thinkers the monument honours — “people who have shaped our thinking across all of history,” as the project puts it — with the serene painted sky overhead.

The library below

Knowledge under one roof

Under the prayer hall spreads the World Peace Library, an expanse the monument gives as about 62,500 square feet. It gathers texts on religion, spirituality, science and philosophy from across the world, with a circular room at its heart devoted to the histories of the major faiths — Judaism, Hinduism, Islam, Christianity, Zoroastrianism and Sikhism among them — set out so that a reader can trace both their differences and what they share. Panels along the walls carry the lives and work of some of the world's great scientists and thinkers.

“Science without religion is lame; religion without science is blind.”
Albert Einstein — a line the founder returns to often

The Idols of Peace

Fifty-four in bronze

The dome's most striking feature is its company of fifty-four bronze figures — saints, scientists, social reformers and philosophers, cast larger than life and gathered, the monument says, under one roof for the first time. Saint Dnyaneshwar and Sant Tukaram stand among them, and so do Mahatma Gandhi, Jesus Christ, Swami Vivekananda, Moses, Hippocrates and the physicist Max Planck. The intent is not devotional but civilisational: to place, in one room, the people “who have helped create civilisation and forward thought as we know it.”

From spirituality to science

The founder's argument

The monument is the physical form of a long essay its founder has written and re-written — that the scriptures of the saints and the equations of the scientists are, read closely, describing the same reality. He points to Dnyaneshwar concluding, three centuries before Galileo, that the sun only appears to move; and to Einstein writing of “the orderly harmony of the universe.” The dome is where that correspondence is meant to be felt rather than merely read.

In 2018 the completed monument hosted a World Parliament of Science, Religion and Philosophy, the kind of gathering it was built to hold. Its ambition is deliberately outsized — an embodiment, the project notes, of the culture of peace and sustainable development “at the heart of UNESCO's mandate.”

In context

A note on the “largest dome” claim

The description most often attached to the monument — the world's largest dome — deserves a footnote rather than a fanfare. By the diameters the site sets side by side, its 160-foot span does exceed the celebrated domes usually cited: St Peter's Basilica in the Vatican at 138 feet, the Pantheon in Rome at 142, and the Gol Gumbaz at Bijapur at 124. Those older domes climb higher or carry more masonry; this one claims the widest clear span. It is a specific, checkable claim, and it is the one the monument makes.
